The Department of International Medical Education studies medical education and international strategy. Its Cancer Immune Therapy Research Center works on new treatments for advanced cancer. The department's head also directs the center.

## International strategy

The unit collects and compares international strategy information from universities in Japan and abroad, so that Nagoya can set a strategy suited to the times. The aim is to raise the university's profile at home and abroad and to promote international joint research through seminars and forums. The work includes joint research within the GAME network, international symposia through consortia such as MIRAI and APRU, and symposia with the partner universities of the joint degree programmes. The GAME and MIRAI pages in the Partnerships section describe the networks.

## Medical education

The unit provides consistent international education, from the medical English seminar in the first year of the undergraduate course, through clinical study abroad in the sixth year, to joint education in the doctoral course. It carries out qualitative research on the graduate education systems of the world's top 100 universities, to train clinical, basic and medical education researchers who can work internationally. The research supports the following activities.

- the medical English seminar;
- the GAME summer camp;
- sending and receiving students for clinical training;
- workshops on clinical practice, research and education.

Recent papers study problem-based learning in English and online, a pharmacology course, and the joint PhD program with the University of Adelaide.

## Cancer Immune Therapy Research Center

The center looks for new treatments of advanced cancer that use the body's own anti-cancer immunity. It has two main topics.

**Oncolytic virus.** An oncolytic virus infects and kills cancer cells while sparing normal cells, and it keeps replicating in neighboring cells. The center uses HF10, a naturally mutated, attenuated herpes simplex virus type 1. Recent studies show that such viruses also change the host's immune response to cancer antigens, which can suppress metastasis. The center studies combinations of HF10 with anticancer drugs and immune checkpoint inhibitors, delivery methods that allow systemic administration, and the use of HF10 as a vector that delivers tumor antigens to engineered T cells.

**Cell therapy.** The center works on T cells and dendritic cells modified genetically to carry receptors that target cancer-specific antigens. It studies a new generation of these cells made from iPS cells. The group builds basic research data and wants to bridge it to clinical trials.
